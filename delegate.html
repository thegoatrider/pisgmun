About PMUN

Podar International School Model United Nations (PMUN) is an academic simulation of the United Nations where students play the role of delegates from different countries and attempt to solve real-world global issues using the policies and perspectives of their assigned nation.

Our Mission

Our mission is to foster a platform of academic excellence, diplomacy, and global citizenship. PMUN encourages young minds to research comprehensively, collaborate productively, and think critically about structural international agendas.

Why Model United Nations?

Model UN helps students build essential leadership, public speaking, negotiation, and research skills. By stepping into the shoes of diplomats, delegates develop historical empathy, learn to build consensus, and understand the complexities of multilateral cooperation.

About Podar International School

Established with a commitment to providing quality education, Podar International School has been at the forefront of experiential learning. PMUN is an extension of this educational vision, empowering students to engage with real-world complexities outside the traditional classroom.

PMUN 2026

PMUN 2026 is designed to challenge delegates in Grades 8, 9, and 10 with graded, age-appropriate scenarios: Grade 8 deals with outer space security (DISEC), Grade 9 handles climate refugees (UNHRC), and Grade 10 tackles modern cyber warfare (UNSC).

Our Philosophy "To cultivate analytical minds capable of addressing systemic international issues with respect, diplomatic compromise, and progressive policies."

A. Committee Setup

A committee is like a discussion room where delegates engage in discussions, debate, and attempt to resolve global issues. Each committee focuses on one main agenda. For example - UNSC, UNICEF etc.

Chair

The Chair in an MUN leads the committee, manages the debate, ensures rules are followed, and helps delegates stay organised and respectful throughout the session.

Vice-Chair

Supports the Chair by helping manage the debate, keeping time, maintaining the speaker's list, and assisting with any procedural matters during the session.

Delegates

The participating students who act as representatives of assigned countries, debating agendas and proposing resolutions.

B. Specific Procedures of PMUN 2026-27

  1. Quorum: A Minimum of 20 delegates will be required to conduct the PMUN in a committee.
  2. The Position Paper is the only paper that needs to be submitted to the Chair.
  3. The Position Paper should be submitted to the Chair of their respective Committees two days before the Conference.
  4. For bloc formation, ensure there are at least three countries in a bloc.
  5. The final Resolution will be passed by a 2/3rd majority in the voting process.

1. Motions

A motion is a formal request made by a delegate during an MUN session to guide or change the flow of the debate. Motions structure the discussion, introduce new topics, or move the committee into different phases of action.

Motion Type Formal Speech Example
1. Motion to Open the General Speakers’ List (GSL) "Motion to open the General Speakers' List for discussion on the agenda."
2. Motion for a Moderated Caucus "Motion for a 10-minute moderated caucus on renewable energy solutions, with 1-minute speaking time."
3. Motion for an Unmoderated Caucus "Motion for a 15-minute unmoderated caucus to draft a resolution."
4. Motion to Close the Speakers’ List "Motion to close the General Speakers' List."
5. Motion to Introduce a Draft Resolution "Motion to introduce Draft Resolution 1.1."
6. Motion to Move into Voting Procedure "Motion to enter Voting Procedure on Draft Resolution 1.1."

2. Points

Points are formal tools used by delegates to address the Chair or the committee, mainly for questions, clarifications, or personal/procedural concerns.

Point Type & Use Formal Speech Example
1. Point of Order
Raised when a delegate believes rules of procedure are being violated.		 "Point of order: The delegate has exceeded the time limit."
2. Point of Personal Privilege
Raised when a delegate experiences personal discomfort (e.g. can’t hear, room too cold).		 "Point of personal privilege: Could the delegate please speak louder?"
3. Point of Parliamentary Inquiry
Asked when a delegate has a question about the rules or what is procedurally allowed.		 "Point of parliamentary inquiry: Can I yield my time to another delegate after my speech?"
4. Point of Information / Inquiry
To ask a question to another delegate, often after they have finished speaking and yielded time.		 "Point of Information: Does the delegate recognise the humanitarian impact of this resolution?"
5. Right of Reply
Requested when a delegate feels their country has been insulted or misrepresented.		 "Right of Reply: The delegate of Country X has made offensive remarks about my nation's government."

3. Yields

In Model United Nations, yields are used when a delegate finishes their formal GSL speech and has remaining speaking time. How you yield that time determines what happens next. Yields are only used during formal speeches.

1. Yield to the Chair

The delegate gives up the remainder of their time. The Chair moves to the next speaker. (Use this if you do not want to take questions or yield to another delegate).

Speech: "With that, I yield the rest of my time to the chair."

2. Yield to Another Delegate

You pass the rest of your speaking time to a delegate of your choice. (The receiving delegate cannot yield it again).

Speech: "I yield the remainder of my time to the delegate of Brazil."

3. Yield to Questions

You allow other delegates to ask you questions. The Chair will open the floor for points of information, and recognize delegates.

Speech: "I now yield the remainder of my time to questions."

Important Notes on Yielding

  • If you run out of time and hear the gavel, you cannot yield; you must yield before your time is up.
  • Yielding to the chair is always the safest option if you are unsure or nervous.

E. Rules of Procedures Detailed

1. Roll Call of Delegates

Roll calls are conducted at the beginning of each session day. The chair announces each country's name alphabetically. After hearing their country, delegates answer with either:

  • Present: You can choose to vote yes, no, or abstain on draft resolutions.
  • Present and Voting: You must vote yes or no on any final resolution. You cannot abstain.

*If a delegate misses roll call, they must send a paper chit to the Chair stating their voting stance.

2. General Speakers' List (GSL)

The default mode of debate. Established after roll call. Shows the order in which delegates deliver their formal Opening Speeches. Delegates raise placards to register to be added to the GSL.

3. Setting the Agenda

The process of voting to decide which topic shortlisted from the study guides will be debated first. Passed by simple majority.

4. Moderated Caucus

A formal, structured caucus enabling rapid discussions on specific sub-issues of the agenda. The proposing delegate must specify: **Total duration** (e.g. 10 mins), **Individual speaking time** (e.g. 60 seconds), and the **Topic**. Requires a simple majority of votes to pass.

5. Unmoderated Caucus & Bloc Building

A temporary suspension of rules allowing delegates to move around, talk freely, build coalition groups (Blocs), and write Draft Resolutions.

Process for Building a Bloc:
1. Find Like-Minded Delegates -> 2. Form a Bloc (at least 3 countries) -> 3. Discuss Solutions -> 4. Draft Resolution.

6. Presenting Draft Resolutions & Amendments

Resolutions outline the proposed solutions. Once drafted, they are submitted to the Chair for review, and then formally read to the committee. Delegates can propose Amendments to clauses:

  • Friendly: Approved by all sponsors. Added automatically.
  • Unfriendly: Not approved by sponsors. Debated and voted upon by the committee.

7. Voting on Draft Resolutions

The final step. All debate ceases. The doors are sealed (no one enters or leaves, absolute silence). Delegates vote by raising placards (In Favour / Against / Abstain). A **2/3rd majority** is required for a resolution to pass and become a **Final Resolution**.

G. PMUN Documents & Samples

A Position Paper outlines your nation's stance, background policy, past actions, and solutions on the agenda. It must be submitted two days before the conference.

Committee: General Assembly (SOCHUM)
Topic: Brain Drain - The Migration of Health Care Workers
Country: Central African Republic (CAR)
Delegate Name: Raj Sharma
School Name: Podar International School, Mumbai
[Introduction/Background]
The Central African Republic, one of the world's poorest nations, faces a severe healthcare crisis driven by brain drain — the emigration of doctors, nurses, and skilled workers to wealthier countries offering better pay and conditions. This has left CAR critically understaffed, with only 0.08 doctors per 1,000 people according to the WHO. The impact is continent-wide, with Africa losing approximately $2 billion annually due to skilled worker migration.

[Country’s Position/Policy]
The Central African Republic recognizes brain drain as a critical threat to healthcare rights. While respecting individuals' freedom to work abroad, CAR stresses the need for stronger support systems to retain health workers within their communities. Wealthy nations must refrain from actively recruiting from already understaffed countries. CAR endorses the WHO Global Code of Practice on fair health worker recruitment, and echoes UN Secretary-General António Guterres' words that "no country can afford to lose its most skilled people, especially in health care, where every worker counts."

[Past International Actions]
The World Health Organization (WHO) created the Global Code of Practice in 2010 to encourage ethical recruitment of health workers. Some countries have also supported African nations with medical training and aid. However, these actions have not been strong enough. Many health workers still leave because their home countries lack proper hospitals, pay, and safety. The United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) and African Union have both highlighted the brain drain as a threat to development and health equality.

[Possible Solutions]
The Central African Republic believes that addressing brain drain requires both local action and international support, and proposes the following measures:
1. Increased international funding to improve hospitals, equipment, and health worker training.
2. Better incentives such as improved pay, housing, and job security to retain health workers locally.
3. Ethical recruitment agreements to prevent developed nations from poaching health workers from shortage-affected countries.
4. Return programs to encourage and support health workers abroad to come back home.
5. Regional cooperation with African nations to establish shared training programs.

[Conclusion]
The Central African Republic believes that health care is a basic human right. We must work together to keep our health workers in the country and improve the system for all. With international support and fair practices, we can reduce brain drain and ensure that every person—no matter where they live—has access to healthcare.

An Opening Speech is the first address you make (1-2 minutes, 150-250 words) to present your position at the start of the conference.

Committee: United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)
Country: Republic of Kenya
Agenda: Strengthening Rural Water Infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa to Advance SDG 6
Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates,

The Republic of Kenya is honoured to participate in this crucial discussion on strengthening rural water infrastructure in Sub-Saharan Africa, in support of achieving Sustainable Development Goal 6.

Access to clean water is not just a human right; it is the foundation for public health, education, agriculture, and gender equality. In Kenya, despite significant progress, millions of people in rural communities still face daily challenges in accessing safe and reliable water.

The Republic of Kenya believes that addressing this issue requires a multi-dimensional approach: improving local water infrastructure, supporting community-based water management, investing in renewable energy solutions for pumping and purification, and most importantly, ensuring international cooperation.

Kenya applauds the efforts of the African Union, UN-Water, and various non-governmental organisations working tirelessly on the ground. However, we urge all member states to increase their financial and technical commitment to building climate-resilient water systems that will serve future generations.

We stand ready to collaborate, share successful models such as our Water Sector Trust Fund, and work with all delegates towards equitable, innovative, and sustainable solutions for rural water access across our continent.

Thank you.
Speaking Tips: Be concise (stick to 1-2 minutes), remain country-specific (represent your assigned nation, not your own opinions), use formal diplomatic language, and deliver with confidence and steady pacing.

A Draft Resolution is a formal proposal. It contains preambulatory clauses (context) and operative clauses (action items).

Common Preambulatory Phrases:
Acknowledging, Affirming, Alarmed, Appreciating, Bearing in mind, Deeply concerned, Emphasising, Having adopted, Noting with concern, Recalling, Recognising, Stressing, Welcoming.
Common Operative Phrases (Numbered):
Calls upon, Condemns, Encourages, Endorses, Invites, Reaffirms, Recommends the adoption of, Requests, Supports, Urges all parties.
Committee: United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF)
Sponsors: India, Bangladesh, Nepal
Signatories: Sri Lanka, Bhutan, Pakistan, Maldives
Draft Resolution Number: DR/1.1
Preambulatory Clauses:
Recognizing that education is a fundamental right of every child as outlined in the Convention on the Rights of the Child,
Concerned by the disparities in access to education in South Asia due to factors including poverty, gender inequality, and geographic remoteness,
Recalling Sustainable Development Goal 4 which aims to "ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all,"

Operative Clauses:
1. Urges member states in South Asia to strengthen national education strategies through community-based awareness initiatives;
2. Establishes the South Asia Regional Education Fund (SAREF) in cooperation with the World Bank and UNESCO to finance school construction and teacher training;
3. Recommends the creation of mobile classrooms using renewable energy to reach children in rural and disaster-affected areas;
4. Calls upon member states to collaborate on a regional teacher training exchange program under the SAARC framework.
